Conflict of Interest

Since reviewers self-select papers to review, maintaining the highest standards of integrity is crucial to the success of a venture such as this.  To this end, please make sure to adhere to this conflict of interest policy in both letter and spirit.

There are two key categories of conflict.  If you, as reviewer, have a direct interest, or can be presumed to have a direct interest, in the material being reviewed, there is an impermissible conflict.  Some indicators of such a conflict are listed below.  In this case, do not write a formal review -- a note in the discussion section is appropriate.

The second category is where you as a reviewer could, by some stretch of imagination, be considered to have a conflict, even though you are pretty confident that you do not.   In such a case, please go ahead and write the review, but make the nature of your association clear, so that the reader can figure out "with how many grains of salt" to read your review.  Examples of this are also given below.

The examples below are necessarily illustrative.  Please use your judgement. If in doubt, please consult with the editorial executive.

Do Not Review

  1. Work you have authored (or co-authored).  If your masterpiece has been unfairly criticized by another reviewer, by all means rebut -- but please do so in the discussion section of digital review.  We, the editorial executive, will provide the links between the original review and the rebuttal (or follow-up).
  2. Work by your Ph.D. thesis advisor or advisee.  This is a life-long conflict -- not just while you have the advisor/advisee relationship.
  3. Work by a colleague -- working for the same institution (company or university) -- even if at a different location/campus.  Applies to current colleagues, immediate past colleagues (within the past year), and possible future colleagues (where recruitment discussions are under way).
  4. Work by a current, recent (within the past year), or prospective (active discussions in progress), collaborator, on a topic related to the collaboration.
  5. Work by a close relative, such as spouse, parent, child, sibling.

Review with Disclosure

  1. Work by an author on whose dissertation committee you were an external examiner.  No disclosure required if ten years have passed since the examination.
  2. Work by an author who served on your dissertation committee at one time, but was not your primary thesis advisor.  No disclosure is required if ten years have passed since the examonation.
  3. Work by an ex-colleague, where you have worked for different institutions for a year or more.  No diclosure is required if five years have passed.
  4. Work by an ex-collaborator.  No disclosure is required if more than five years have passed since the most recent appearance of any fruits of your collaboration.
  5. Work by a current collaborator on a topic unrelated to your collaboration.
  6. Work by a distant relative, such as cousin.
  7. Work by an ex-relative, such as an ex-spouse.

Example Disclosures

  1. "The author and I were in the same department, but never worked together, and have been with different employers for the past two years. I had no hand in the work being reviewed, and believe that I have no bias on account of my previous proximity to the author."
  2. "The second author and I have collaborated on a number of topics over the years. Unfortunately, this paper (and the related stream of research) is not one of them (:-(. Since I played no part in the development of the ideas in the stream of research represented by this paper, I believe I can evaluate this work without bias."
